Why this recommendation

HOCl

15 × (800 ÷ 200) = 60 min assumes constant concentration × time. This exceeds the studied 1–15 minute window; effectiveness and germination at this duration were not established.

H₂O₂

5 × (3 ÷ 12) = 1.25 min assumes constant concentration × time. At 12%, that is 75 seconds, an untested extrapolation. It does not predict germination or microbial reduction. The separate 12% × 15-minute broccoli bacterial experiment is documented in the research note; it is not the timing-model anchor.

Daily sprays

Peroxide spray research tested plant tolerance, not a reliable daily mold-prevention dose.

Seed treatment reduces some contamination; it does not establish that a crop is safe to eat. Genuine mold in an edible tray → discard the batch.

What tissue culture adds

Borrow the process controls. Consistent seed contact, clean handling and seed-lot checks can improve the evaluation. Culture methods do not validate a new edible-microgreen dose.

  • Check the lot: internal microbes and seed condition can limit what a surface bath achieves.
  • Measure growth too: less contamination can come with seed injury. Track germination and usable harvest.
  • Keep endpoints separate: a clean culture is not a measured pathogen log reduction. Longer exposure does not always produce a larger reduction.

How the unconfirmed times are calculated

The HOCl estimate assumes equal concentration × time: time = 15 min × 800 ppm ÷ selected ppm. It is an arithmetic comparison anchored to the broccoli experiment. It does not predict equivalent microbial reduction or germination. Free chlorine loss, pH, seed debris and seed type can change the outcome.

How the inputs affect the result

More finished batch increases stock and water in proportion. A stronger stock needs less stock for the same target. A higher target needs more stock; the calculator cannot predict mold control or germination. Check actual concentration after mixing and follow the label for contact time and rinsing.

For weight percentages, weigh both stock and water: stock mass = target% ÷ stock% × final mass. For volume percentages use matching volume units. If a bottle says % by weight, its volume is not interchangeable with mass. HOCl uses measured available chlorine in mg/L (ppm) and mL; add water to the final volume.

What about 12% peroxide and longer soaks?

The study selected 12% H₂O₂ × 15 minutes in its broccoli bacterial screen; its fungal comparison used 3%. Neither validates 12% on sunflower/peas or daily sprays. No tested concentration–time equivalence is established here: weaker solution does not justify a calculated longer soak. Study methods and results ↗

No universal chemical “winner” is established across all seeds. Research results, grower practice and your product’s directions answer different questions.

Why no routine daily spray?

No daily HOCl or H₂O₂ concentration is recommended here for these five coir-grown crops. Peroxide research applied 0–200 ppm once daily to sunflower and radish to assess plant damage and growth, not mold-control effectiveness. Peas, broccoli and mung were outside that trial.

A radish-sprout study used 30 and 50 ppm available chlorine during sprouting. Sprout conditions and application details do not establish a daily spray for covered coir trays. Neither study supplies a routine spray concentration for all five seeds.

Sources & evidence limits

Crop priorities reflect handling advice. Dilution arithmetic calculates quantities; it cannot predict mold control, germination or an effective contact time. Use measured concentration and product-specific directions.

  • Gilbert, Diaz & Bregoff (2023), Foods — broccoli disinfestation experiments; not a validation for all microgreen crops.
  • Bootstrap Farmer: seed disinfection — grower practice and contact-time guidance.
  • Bootstrap Farmer: soaking seeds — large-seed handling context.
  • Penn State: microgreen food safety — seed, water and hygiene context.
  • Eicher-Sodo: peroxide phytotoxicity research — daily spray tolerance and growth, not demonstrated mold control.
  • Electrolyzed water on radish sprouts (2016) — different production conditions from covered coir microgreens.
