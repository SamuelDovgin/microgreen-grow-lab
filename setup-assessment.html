Assessment of the pasted MGM lighting setup

Reviewed October 30, 2026. Configuration is the user’s pasted lighting-page snapshot, preserved locally in the source notebook. This assessment distinguishes published practice, crop experiments and our uncalibrated model.

Judgment: a reasonable low-cost starting setup for a crop trial, with limited light-output headroom and unresolved uniformity. It is not demonstrated to be a bad setup, and it is not verified as an all-crop optimum. If the user wants every tray contained, the 18-inch shelf does not satisfy that requirement.

Follow-up: should the light dose be increased?

The user’s later custom snapshot uses three 22 W bars, 7-inch adjacent spacing, 18-inch depth and the same 5.445-inch modeled gap. It estimates 139.35 median PPFD / 8.03 DLI at 16 hours. That is a different baseline from the original two-bar setup assessed below.

Recommendation: compare the current dose with a modest 9–10 DLI treatment before pursuing 12–16. This is an editorial trial recommendation informed by the evidence, not a demonstrated optimum. At 16 h/day, 9, 10 and 12 DLI require 156.25, 173.61 and 208.33 PPFD respectively. Moving from 8 to 9–10 increases daily photons by about 12.5–25%. A higher photon dose is not a promise of a proportional yield increase.

Possible outcome of extra light What the evidence supports
Greater fresh harvest Possible in responsive crops. Cornell’s arugula/mizuna/mustard study found increases with higher DLI, with diminishing arugula gains near the upper end. Other experiments found little or no yield benefit in selected crops.
More dry matter, shorter stems Supported by several Brassica experiments. This may help where shoots are overly elongated; it can also produce stems that are less convenient to cut.
Earlier harvest Cornell observed shorter time to its defined harvest stage with increasing DLI in those three crops. The number of days saved cannot be transferred to this rack.
Pigments and phenolic composition Can change with more light, with direction and magnitude depending on crop and compound. More light does not consistently increase every pigment or every quality measure.
Energy use Higher power or longer lighting hours raise lighting electricity. The relevant comparison is usable yield, form and harvest timing per kWh, rather than attaining a higher badge value.

Allred’s Cornell thesis (L33) tested DLI 3/6/9/12 crossed with CO₂ 400/600/800/1000 ppm. At 400 ppm, mizuna/mustard fresh mass rose 27%/35% from DLI 3 to 12; arugula increased 21% from 6 to 9 but only 5% from 9 to 12. These are crop-specific results from cells and defined harvest stages, not predicted percentage gains for the user’s trays. The author also observed shorter time to harvest as DLI increased.

A 2024 controlled Brassica study (L34) compared 120/160/210 PPFD at 16 h/day. Higher intensity generally shortened stems and increased dry matter in several entries, while fresh yield often stayed similar or was greater at the lower intensities. Color and chemical responses differed by cultivar. This is why extra dry matter should not be equated with extra fresh saleable grams.

The most useful comparison is the same crop/seed lot at the current intensity versus 9–10 DLI, with density, medium, nutrition, watering and room conditions held similar. Use replicate trays and repeat a sowing when feasible. Record usable grams, stem height, form/color, harvest day and light kWh. The automatic values are uncalibrated; use canopy readings when possible to establish the actual difference.

Keeping 16 h/day and raising intensity isolates the lighting-intensity change. Alternatively, at the current estimated 139.35 PPFD, 18 h/day gives 9.03 DLI without more fixture power, but photoperiod changes may affect development independently; equal DLI does not guarantee equivalent crop results. Avoid extending to continuous light solely to chase the reference band.

What was assessed

  • Nominal 48 × 18-inch shelf, four nominal 10 × 20-inch planted canopies.
  • Two 46.1-inch, 22 W bars; 14-inch modeled center spacing.
  • 10-inch deck pitch, 1.78-inch assumed fixture drop, 1.275-inch medium elevation and 1.5-inch representative shoots.
  • Calculated 5.445-inch leaf-to-LED gap, 44 W/shelf, five lit shelves and 16 h/day.
  • Automatic model: 80.97 median / 82.11 mean PPFD; sampled range 33.88–115.25. Median DLI 4.66, mean DLI 4.73; seven-day median dose 32.65 mol/m². These are estimates, not measured crop readings.

What is supported

The basic two-bar system is credible. MGM’s arugula article documents two 22 W T5 LEDs per shelf, 10-inch shelf spacing and 16 h on / 8 h off as conditions at his operating farm. This is relevant practical experience, but it is self-reported creator evidence, not a controlled lighting comparison or a canopy measurement. Our exact 18-inch rack/production tray count and bar coordinates are assembled from separate sources. [L05]

The reference-band warning does not establish failure. Virginia Tech’s DLI guide lists 9–12 for microgreens; its production guide gives 9–16 generally but also lists species-specific work using 70–90 PPFD and 14–16-hour schedules for cabbage/Chinese kale. Thus the general band should be treated as a trial reference, not a universal biological minimum. At 16 hours, 70–90 PPFD corresponds arithmetically to 4.03–5.18 DLI. [L01,L02]

Liu et al. (2022) tested cabbage/Chinese kale under 30–90 PPFD in one experiment and different photoperiods at 70 PPFD in another. Their results support cultivation at relatively low intensity with crop- and endpoint-dependent responses. The two experiments do not validate every intensity × photoperiod combination, white-shop-light spectrum, or our exact shelf. [L31]

The archived de Lima et al. (2026) full paper compared 50/100/150/200 PPFD with three seeding densities and three replicates per combination in four species experiments. Its results section reports no significant PPFD main effect on fresh/dry yield for sunflower, radish or red cabbage; green cabbage responded. This supports testing lower light for those particular crops, not assuming equivalent quality or yield for all cultivars and systems. [L32]

Where this setup is weak

Issue Assessment
Light quantity Potentially lean. The model’s ~4.7 DLI is below the general reference, so there may be limited margin for crops that benefit from more light. Actual output is not known.
Uniformity Worth investigating. The modeled 34–115 PPFD range and 41% minimum/mean indicate weak regions under this geometry. Exact ratios depend on an unmeasured beam pattern; they do not establish actual tray variation.
Clearance 5.45 inches is not an automatic failure. Our 6–10-inch message is an editorial starting guide. Under the same offsets, 3-inch shoots leave 3.945 inches and 5-inch shoots leave 1.945 inches. Tall crops need their own spacing check.
Tray fit The representative 21.25-inch outer rim overhangs 18-inch depth by 1.625 inches front/back. This fails a contained-tray preference; it does not itself establish structural failure.
Energy At the entered $0.15/kWh, lights cost $15.84 per 30 days for the fully lit rack. This is lighting-only arithmetic, not proof of low cost per saleable harvest.

How much to trust the automatic verdict

The current estimate borrows Hykolity’s 100 lm/W rating and Iowa State’s rough divide-by-60 photon conversion. Actual spectral output and angular distribution are unknown. Reflections, side emission and daylight are omitted. The median and mean happen to be close here, so changing the summary statistic would not remove the low-reference result: 4.66 versus 4.73 DLI. [L11,L28]

An output-budget check helps explain the concern: under the same conversion assumptions, two 2200-lumen bars emit approximately 73.33 µmol/s. Four 10 × 20-inch canopies total 0.516128 m². Even if all estimated photons reached them, the area-average upper bound would be approximately 142 PPFD / 8.18 DLI at 16 h. This is conditional on an uncertain PPF estimate, not an experimentally established ceiling for the real lamps. Repositioning cannot create additional photons.

What I would do

Keep the 16-hour schedule and test one shelf before buying or reproducing a whole rack. Use crop performance as the deciding evidence: harvested usable mass, harvest timing, stem form/color and variation among trays, plus electricity. A stronger light level is useful only if the crop result justifies it.

For the user’s no-overhang preference, use the 24-inch shelf and reconsider bar placement. Do not automatically move two bars out to 20-inch separation. At the same 5.445-inch gap, our model gives ~40 median PPFD with 20-inch separation, compared with ~84 at 12-inch separation (centers about 6 and 18 inches). These are illustrative comparisons, not measured or optimized mounting coordinates. Three distributed 20–22 W bars offer more placement/output headroom if a two-bar crop trial is unsatisfactory; they are not guaranteed to reach 9–12 DLI.

Practical decision: reasonable as an inexpensive trial for short-cycle crops; less convincing as a fixed design for all species or maximum throughput. The clearest improvement for this user is contained tray fit and thoughtful lamp placement, followed by a crop-specific comparison—not buying more lights solely because the generic model badge says “below.”
